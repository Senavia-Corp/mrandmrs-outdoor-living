A Florida backyard gets used when it is designed for the climate first. That means shade where people actually sit, protection from afternoon rain and insects, materials that shrug off sun, humidity and salt, and utilities run before anything is built on top of them. Everything else, the furniture, the fire feature, the finishes, is decoration on that frame. This guide is the order we design in, and the reasons behind it.

## Design for the climate, then for the look

Four facts drive every decision:

- **Sun.** Direct afternoon sun makes an unshaded Florida patio unusable for much of the year. Shade is not an accessory; it is what makes the space a room.
- **Rain.** Summer afternoons bring short, heavy rain. A covered area that stays dry keeps a dinner going; an uncovered one ends it.
- **Insects.** Screening is the difference between an evening outside and an evening indoors looking at the patio.
- **Wind.** Structures, screens and roofs are engineered to the wind design for your location under the Florida Building Code. A structure that is not is both a permit problem and a hazard.

Humidity and salt air decide the materials, which we cover below.

## The order to build in

Backyards built out of order get dug up. The sequence that avoids it:

1. **Drainage and grading.** Where water goes when it rains, before anything sits on the ground.
2. **Utilities.** Gas to the kitchen and the pool heater, electrical to the equipment pad, the kitchen and the lighting, water and drain to the kitchen sink, conduit for automation and screens. Sleeves under future decks and walkways even for phases you have not committed to.
3. **The pool.** It is the largest excavation and the structure everything else is placed around.
4. **Structures.** Pergola, louvered roof, patio cover or screen enclosure, with footings placed off the pool plumbing.
5. **The kitchen.** On its utilities, under cover.
6. **Decking, finishes, lighting, landscape.** Last, because they are what the earlier work would damage.

## Shade: pergola, louvered roof or cover

The three shade structures solve different problems, and many backyards use two.

| Structure | What it does | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Aluminum pergola | Filtered shade, structure to hang lights and fans, defines the room | Dining and lounge areas that want sky; the pool edge |
| Motorized louvered roof | Shade you can open, and a closed roof in rain | The area where you will eat and sit through a storm |
| Solid patio cover or lanai roof | Full shade and rain protection, a ceiling for fans and lights | The transition from the house to the pool |

The deciding questions are what you do under it and whether rain matters there. [Pergola vs. louvered roof](/blogs/pergola-vs-louvered-roof) walks through the choice; [aluminum vs. wood pergolas](/blogs/aluminum-vs-wood-pergolas-in-florida) explains why aluminum is the usual answer in this climate.

## Screens: rooms, enclosures and motorized screens

Insect protection changes how much the space is used more than any finish does. The options are a fixed screen room or patio enclosure, a pool screen enclosure over the pool and deck, or motorized retractable screens that close a pergola or lanai on demand and disappear otherwise. A pool enclosure can also serve as the barrier that Florida's pool safety law requires. ## The outdoor kitchen

An outdoor kitchen is a room, not an appliance. It needs cover, counter space on both sides of the grill, a sink if you will actually cook rather than only grill, lighting over the work surface, and a layout that lets the cook see the pool and the table. The material decisions are about humidity and salt: masonry or framed islands with weather-rated cladding, marine-grade stainless hardware, and appliances built for outdoor use. We cover the choices in [best outdoor kitchen materials for Florida](/blogs/best-outdoor-kitchen-materials-for-florida) and the layout in [the outdoor kitchen layout guide](/blogs/outdoor-kitchen-layout-guide).

## Materials that survive here

| Element | Choose | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Deck and walkways | Travertine or pavers on a compacted base; concrete finished for traction | Thin coatings over bad base; untreated wood at the water's edge |
| Structures | Aluminum engineered for the local wind design, powder-coated | Timber without rated hardware and footings |
| Kitchen | Masonry or framed islands, weather-rated cladding, outdoor-rated appliances, 316 or marine-grade stainless near salt | Indoor appliances, uncovered cabinetry, cheap stainless |
| Screens | Aluminum framing and screen mesh rated for the exposure | Mesh and frames sized for a milder climate |
| Pool finishes and fixtures | Finishes chosen with the sanitizer in mind; marine-grade metals | Metals that corrode in salt water or salt air |
| Furniture and fabrics | Powder-coated aluminum, teak, solution-dyed acrylic fabrics | Untreated wood, fabrics that fade and hold water |

## Lighting and controls

Design lighting in layers: the pool and spa lit as a composition, task light over the kitchen, ambient light under the pergola or roof, and path light where people walk. Put all of it on one control with the pool equipment, so the backyard turns on with one tap. The conduit for every circuit goes in during step 2 above.

## Landscape that does not fight the pool

Plant away from the water's edge with species that do not drop into the pool, keep root systems away from the shell and the plumbing, and use irrigation that does not spray the deck or the screens. Privacy comes from layered planting and screens rather than a wall of one species. The rule is simple: anything that drops, sheds or seeks water goes elsewhere.

## Bringing it together

The backyards that work are designed as one project even when they are built in phases: the pool, the structures, the kitchen and the screens placed together so that footings miss plumbing, shade lands where people sit, and the utilities for phase three go in during phase one.
