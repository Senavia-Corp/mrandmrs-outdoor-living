A commercial pool in Florida is a public pool in the eyes of the state, and that changes everything about how it is designed, permitted, built and run. It needs a construction permit from the Florida Department of Health in addition to the local building permit, it is engineered to the public pool rule rather than to the owner's preferences, and it carries operating obligations from the day it opens. For a developer, an HOA board or a property manager, the decisions that matter are made before design starts.

## What counts as a commercial pool

Florida's Department of Health regulates pools that serve the public under Chapter 64E-9 of the Florida Administrative Code. That covers hotel and resort pools, condominium and apartment community pools, club and HOA pools, and pools at schools and similar facilities. A few narrow exemptions exist for very small communities and certain private arrangements; the Department makes the determination, and assuming a community pool is "residential" because the residents own it is the first mistake we see. If people other than one household will swim in it, plan it as a public pool until the Department says otherwise.

## Two permits, two agencies

A residential pool has one permit from the local building department. A public pool has two:

- **The building permit**, issued by the city or county under the Florida Building Code, covering the structure, the electrical, the plumbing and the deck, with the usual inspection sequence.
- **The Department of Health construction permit**, issued after the Department reviews engineered plans against the public pool rule. The Department inspects before opening and issues the operating permit that allows the pool to be used.

The two reviews run in parallel and each has its own submittal. Our guide to [permits for pool construction in Florida](/blogs/what-permits-are-required-for-pool-construction-in-florida) covers the building-permit side; the Department's side is specific to public pools and is handled by the engineer and the contractor.

## What the public pool rule decides for you

On a residential pool the owner chooses almost everything. On a public pool, the rule specifies much of it:

- **Hydraulics and filtration**: turnover rate, filter sizing, flow metering, and the circulation layout.
- **Water treatment and control**: disinfection, pH control and the monitoring the operator will have to keep up.
- **Depths, slopes and markings**: depth markers, no-diving markings, and the profile of the pool floor.
- **Decks and drainage**: deck width, slope and drainage so water does not return to the pool.
- **Suction outlets**: anti-entrapment covers and the dual-outlet or equivalent protection the rule and federal law require.
- **Lighting, ladders, handrails, lifelines and signage.**
- **Equipment room and chemical storage** requirements.

None of these are design preferences. They are what the engineer draws to, and what the Department checks.

## Accessibility

Pools at public accommodations are subject to the federal accessibility standards. For most new pools that means an accessible means of entry, typically a pool lift or a sloped entry, placed and specified in the design. Retrofitting access after the deck is poured is more expensive than designing it in, and an opening-day inspection that finds it missing is a delay you can avoid.

## Operating obligations after opening

A public pool is inspected by the Department after it opens, and the operator is responsible for water quality records, functioning safety equipment, signage, and keeping the pool within the rule. Whoever will manage the property should be part of the design conversation, because decisions like automated chemical control, equipment-room layout and access for service determine how hard the pool is to run.

## Planning a commercial pool project

The order that avoids expensive revisions:

1. **Classification.** Confirm with the Department of Health that the pool is public, and which category.
2. **Program.** Who uses it, how many at once, what it is for: lap swimming, leisure, a resort amenity, a community pool. This drives size, depth and features.
3. **Engineering.** A Florida-licensed engineer experienced in public pools draws to the rule. This is where the hydraulics, treatment and accessibility are fixed.
4. **Permits.** Building permit and Department of Health construction permit, in parallel.
5. **Construction.** Inspected by both agencies at the stages each requires.
6. **Opening.** Department inspection, operating permit, and the hand-off to the operator with the records and training they need.

## Where the budget goes

We do not publish per-square-foot figures for commercial pools because they mislead: two pools of the same size can differ several-fold in cost depending on the treatment system, the deck program, accessibility, the equipment room and the site. What is consistent is that a public pool costs more than a residential pool of the same size, because the rule specifies more, both agencies inspect it, and the engineering is heavier. A realistic budget comes from a program and an engineered concept, which is what we prepare at the start of a commercial engagement.
