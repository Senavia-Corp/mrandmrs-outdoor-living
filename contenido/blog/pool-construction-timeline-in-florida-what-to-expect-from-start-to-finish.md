A pool in Florida is built in a fixed order, and the calendar is set by what each phase waits on: the permit review, the weather, an inspection, or the cure time of concrete and plaster. Crews can be fast; they cannot shorten a cure or skip an inspection. So instead of a week-by-week schedule that would be wrong for your lot, this guide gives you the sequence, what gates each phase, and where projects really gain or lose time.

## Before anything is dug

Two phases happen before the yard changes, and they decide most of the schedule.

**Design and engineering.** Site evaluation, the 3D design you approve, selections, and the sealed structural plans. The time here is mostly decision time on your side: every selection locked before engineering is a change order avoided later. Our guide to [the twelve decisions to make before excavation](/blogs/before-you-build-a-pool-in-florida) is the checklist we use.

**Permitting.** The building department reviews the plans; zoning, the HOA, and where they apply the septic and flood-zone reviews run alongside. We cover [what each permit is and who issues it](/blogs/what-permits-are-required-for-pool-construction-in-florida). The length of this phase depends on the office and the season, and the fastest way through it is a complete submission the first time.

## The construction phases, in order

| Phase | What happens | What it waits on | What gates the next phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Layout and excavation | Shape marked, hole dug to the engineered profile, spoil hauled | Permit issued, dry ground, access path open | Correct depth and profile; rock or water table handled |
| 2. Plumbing and steel | Lines run and pressure-tested, rebar grid tied, bonding grid tied in | Excavation complete | Inspection passes |
| 3. Shell | Gunite or shotcrete applied over the steel, then cured | Steel and plumbing inspection | Cure time (not adjustable) |
| 4. Tile and coping | Waterline tile and coping set on the cured shell | Shell cured | Mortar cure before deck work on the edge |
| 5. Electrical rough-in and deck | Conduit, lighting, equipment circuits; deck formed and poured or pavers set | Tile and coping done | Electrical and deck inspections |
| 6. Equipment | Pump, filter, heater, chlorination, automation, lighting installed and wired | Deck and electrical inspections | Power to the pad |
| 7. Interior finish and fill | Plaster, quartz or pebble applied; pool filled immediately | All dusty work finished, inspections closed | Startup chemistry over the first days |
| 8. Startup, barrier, final | Systems run and tested, chemistry balanced, barrier complete, final inspections | Everything above | Permit closed; pool in use |

Phases 5 and 6 can overlap, and the barrier can be built while the finish cures. The three things that cannot move are the inspections (steel before shell, rough-in before the deck, barrier before the final), the shell cure, and the plaster cure.

## Where the time really goes

From our projects, the calendar is lost or won in a short list of places.

- **The permit package.** A missing HOA approval, an easement left off the site plan, or a septic question discovered late costs more days than any construction phase.
- **Rain.** Excavation, gunite and deck pours stop for wet ground. A summer week can lose several working days to afternoon storms; a tropical system can pause a site entirely.
- **The water table and rock.** On a high water table, the excavation needs dewatering before steel and shell; on North Florida lots, limestone can slow the dig and change the method. Both are predictable from the site evaluation, which is why we do it first.
- **Selections.** A tile or finish that has not been chosen when the shell is cured is the most common self-inflicted delay.
- **Inspection scheduling.** Each stage is inspected when the inspector is available. A failed item, often a bonding connection or a gate latch, means a re-inspection.

## Seasons

Florida builds pools year-round, but not evenly. The dry season, late fall through spring, is the predictable window for excavation and concrete, and it is the busy one: design and permitting for a dry-season build should start well ahead of it. Summer builds are possible and sometimes faster to permit; they carry more weather days and the hurricane-season risk of a pause.

## Startup is not the finish line

A plastered pool is filled immediately and then balanced over the first days while the finish cures underwater. During that window the equipment runs on a startup schedule and the water chemistry is adjusted daily. We walk you through the equipment and the automation at startup, hand over the closed permit, and the pool is yours. The barrier, the one thing the final inspection will not pass without, is built in parallel, not after.
