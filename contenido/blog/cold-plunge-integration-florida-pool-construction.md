A cold plunge built with a new pool is a small vessel on its own loop: its own pump, its own filter, its own sanitation and a chiller sized to hold cold water in a climate that does everything it can to warm it. It can sit beside the spa, share the equipment pad and run from the same controller, and it can look like part of the pool. What it cannot do is share the pool's water or be added easily after the deck is poured. This guide covers what we settle with clients who want one, in the order it comes up.

## Why it is a separate system

A Florida pool runs in the 80s for much of the summer, and a spa runs around 100°F. A cold plunge is meant to sit decades below both, so none of the three can share water. A plunge that is plumbed into the pool loop is simply a cold spot that the pool warms up in minutes.

That is why a built-in plunge gets:

- **Its own circulation pump**, sized to its volume, usually variable-speed.
- **Its own filter and sanitation.** The water is small in volume and used by one or two people at a time, but it is still a body of water that needs filtering and sanitizing to the same standard as the pool.
- **A chiller.** A heat-pump chiller pulls heat out of the water. Some units heat and cool, which lets the same vessel run as a warm soak in the North Florida winter.
- **Insulation.** The shell, the plumbing runs and the cover decide how hard the chiller works. A plunge in full sun on a travertine deck is a very different load from one under a lanai roof with an insulated cover.

What it can share is the equipment pad, the trench from the pad to the house, the electrical feed, the automation controller and, visually, the finish, tile and coping of the pool.

## Sizing the chiller for Florida

Chiller capacity is the decision that separates a plunge that holds 50°F in August from one that creeps into the 60s by the afternoon. The load depends on the volume of water, the temperature you want, the surface area exposed to air and sun, how well the shell and plumbing are insulated, and how often the cover is off. We size it from those numbers for your lot, with a margin for the hottest weeks, rather than from the vessel's catalog volume alone. Undersizing is the mistake we see most in plunges added later, because the only way to fix it is to replace the chiller.

## Electrical

A chiller is a dedicated electrical load, and a plunge adds its own pump and often a small heater. All of it is permitted with the pool's electrical, bonded and installed to the code's pool and spa provisions, with the panel capacity confirmed before the pad is set. Running the circuit later means opening a finished deck or wall. This is the same point our guide to [upgrades worth planning before construction](/blogs/pool-upgrades-worth-planning-before-construction) makes about heaters and automation: the equipment can wait, the capacity and the conduit cannot.

## Placement, entry and safety

Where the plunge goes decides how much it is used and how safe it is.

- **Next to the spa** is the usual answer: the two are used together, they share the pad and the trench, and the contrast is the point.
- **Entry.** Cold water and a tall vessel are a bad combination. We design steps, a handrail and a seat so the user is never climbing out of cold water unsupported. The vessel depth is set so an adult can sit with shoulders under and feet on the floor.
- **Shade** cuts the chiller load and keeps the deck around the vessel cooler to stand on.
- **The barrier.** A built-in plunge is a body of water inside the pool barrier required by Florida's Residential Swimming Pool Safety Act, with the same gate, alarm or cover requirements as the pool. A lockable, insulated cover does double duty: it holds the cold and it closes the vessel when nobody is around.

## Built in or placed on the deck

A plunge can be a gunite vessel finished to match the pool, a prefabricated shell set into the deck, or a freestanding tub placed on the deck after the fact. The three are not the same project:

| | Built-in gunite or set-in shell | Freestanding tub on the deck |
|---|---|---|
| When it is decided | With the pool, before the shell | Any time |
| Equipment | Own pump, filter, sanitation and chiller on the pad | Integrated in the unit, plugged in nearby |
| Permits | Part of the pool permit and barrier | Depends on size and electrical; ask the building department |
| Look | Matches the pool's finish, tile and coping | A separate object on the deck |
| Capacity in Florida heat | Sized for the lot | Fixed by the product |

We build the first kind, and we will tell you if the second is the better answer for your situation.

## What to settle before the shell is shot

1. **Whether you want one.** It is a commitment to a vessel, a chiller and a loop, not a feature that can be switched on later.
2. **Where it sits** relative to the spa, the shade and the equipment pad.
3. **Depth and entry**: who will use it and how they get in and out.
4. **Chiller capacity** from the real load, with the electrical panel confirmed.
5. **Cover and insulation**, specified with the vessel, not bought afterwards.
6. **Control.** The plunge runs from the same automation as the pool, with its own schedule and set point.

## Who should not use one

Cold water immersion is a stress on the heart and breathing, and that is the point of it for the people who use it. It is also why anyone with a cardiovascular condition, who is pregnant, or who has another health concern should talk to a physician first, and why it should not be used alone. We build the vessel to be safe to enter and leave; how it is used is between the owner and their doctor.
