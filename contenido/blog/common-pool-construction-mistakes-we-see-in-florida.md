Most of the pool problems we are asked to fix in Florida were decided before the first shovel. The lot was not evaluated, the water was not planned for, the plumbing was sized by habit, the deck was poured flat, or the barrier was left until the inspector asked for it. None of these are exotic. They are the ordinary mistakes of building a pool as if the site did not matter, and in Florida the site always matters.

This is the list we use when we assess an existing pool for a complete remodel, and the list we design against on a new one.

## 1. Designing before the lot has been evaluated

A pool is engineered for a specific piece of ground. Water table, drainage pattern, rock, the septic drainfield, easements and the path the excavator will take all change the design or the price. When a pool is designed from a catalog shape and the lot is discovered during the dig, the result is a change order, a redesign, or a pool that is simply in the wrong place.

What prevents it: a site evaluation before the design, with the tank and drainfield located, the easements and setbacks drawn, and a probe for rock where the county geology suggests it. Our guide to [what to decide before you build](/blogs/before-you-build-a-pool-in-florida) starts there.

## 2. Ignoring the water table

Much of Florida has groundwater close to the surface. Two things follow. During construction, an excavation that fills with water has to be dewatered before steel and shell can go in, and that has to be in the plan. After construction, an empty concrete shell can be pushed up by groundwater, which is why the engineering includes a hydrostatic relief valve and why a pool should never be drained without a professional managing it.

What prevents it: knowing the water table before the design, engineering for it, and never draining the pool "to clean it" without advice.

## 3. Hitting rock without a plan

In North Central Florida, limestone sits close to the surface on many lots around Gainesville and Ocala. A dig that expects sand and finds rock slows down, changes method, and costs more. That is manageable when it is expected and expensive when it is a surprise.

What prevents it: a probe or soil evaluation on lots where the county geology suggests rock, and a contract that says how rock excavation is priced before the dig. We explain the North Florida specifics in [building a pool in Gainesville or Ocala](/blogs/building-a-pool-in-north-florida).

## 4. Plumbing sized by habit

Suction and return lines, the number of returns, the skimmer count and the pump are a system. When the lines are sized to what the crew always uses rather than to the pool's volume and the features it has, the result is a pool that never quite clears, a pump that runs harder than it should, and a spa that takes too long to heat.

What prevents it: hydraulics designed for the pool, with the spa, water features and automation included, before the plumbing goes in the ground. This is also where the automation conduit and valve actuators are placed, because they cannot be added cheaply later.

## 5. Bonding and electrical done as an afterthought

The electrical code requires everything conductive around the water to be bonded into one grid, and the inspector has to see that grid before the deck covers it. A missed bonding connection is one of the most common inspection failures we hear about, and a deck that has already been poured over it is an expensive one.

What prevents it: an electrical plan reviewed with the pool plan, and the rough-in inspection passed before any concrete goes down.

## 6. A deck that drains the wrong way

The deck has to shed rain away from the house and away from the pool. A flat deck or one pitched toward the coping sends dirt and runoff into the water and leaves puddles that stain stone and grow algae. On paver decks, an inadequate base settles and the pavers rock.

What prevents it: deck grading designed with the drainage plan, deck drains where the layout traps water, and a base built for the paver.

## 7. The wrong finish for the water chemistry

Interior finishes age differently with salt systems, with hard well water and with unbalanced chemistry. A finish chosen on color alone, or a salt system added to a finish that was not specified for it, shows up as staining, etching or early roughness. The same is true of metal fixtures and some natural-stone copings in salt water.

What prevents it: choosing the finish, the coping and the sanitizer together, and starting the pool up on a proper schedule after plaster.

## 8. The barrier left for the end

Florida's Residential Swimming Pool Safety Act requires a safety feature, most often a barrier or screen enclosure meeting the Building Code's pool barrier rules, before the final inspection. When the barrier is treated as the last item, the finished pool sits unused while a gate latch, a door alarm or a fence gap is corrected.

What prevents it: designing the barrier with the pool. Our [permits guide](/blogs/what-permits-are-required-for-pool-construction-in-florida) explains what the code asks of it.

## 9. Features that were not planned for

A sun shelf with no bubbler conduit, a spa with no spillway, lighting with no transformer capacity, a pergola whose footings land on the pool plumbing. Each is cheap at the design table and expensive once the shell exists.

What prevents it: deciding the features you may want before engineering, even the ones you will add later. Our guide to [upgrades worth planning before construction](/blogs/pool-upgrades-worth-planning-before-construction) is the list.

## 10. Hiring on price alone

Every item above is more likely when the builder was chosen on the lowest number. A low price is often a scope that leaves out engineering, the barrier, the deck drainage or the permit itself, and the difference reappears as change orders.

What prevents it: a fixed project price in writing after the design, a verified license, the engineer's name, and the inspection record. Our list of [questions to ask before you sign](/blogs/how-to-choose-a-pool-builder-in-florida) is built around this.

## If your pool already has one of these

Most of the items above can be corrected inside a complete remodel: plumbing and equipment replaced, decks regraded and resurfaced, electrical brought to code, and the barrier made compliant.
