A new inground pool in Florida needs one building permit from the city or county that has jurisdiction over your property. That permit covers the pool shell, the deck, the electrical work and the plumbing, each inspected at its own stage, and it is not closed until the safety barrier passes. Depending on your lot, zoning, your HOA, a septic system or a flood zone add approvals of their own. The licensed contractor applies for all of it under their license.

That is the short answer. The rest of this guide explains who issues what, what the plans have to show, what the inspectors look at, and the two things that most often hold a pool permit up.

## Who issues pool permits in Florida, and who does not

Florida splits the job in two. The state writes the rules and licenses the people who build; your local government issues the permit and checks the work.

- **The Florida Building Commission** publishes the Florida Building Code. The 8th Edition (2023) took effect on December 31, 2023, and it is the edition under which current pool permits are reviewed. Residential pools fall under the Residential volume's swimming-pool chapter, which is where barrier, deck, suction-outlet and construction requirements live.
- **The Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR)** licenses pool and spa contractors through the Construction Industry Licensing Board. A pool built by a licensed contractor is permitted under that license. You can check any license number on the DBPR site; ours are published on every page of this website.
- **Your city or county building department** receives the application, reviews the plans, issues the permit, performs the inspections and closes the permit. Inside city limits that is usually the city (Gainesville, Ocala); outside, the county (Alachua, Marion). The county you are in decides which portal you use and which local amendments apply.

What the state does not do is issue the permit. There is no statewide pool permit. If a contractor tells you the permit is "handled at the state level," ask again.

## What the building permit application covers

A pool permit application is a package, and the review cannot start until the package is complete. For a custom inground pool it normally includes:

- **A site plan** showing the lot, the house, the pool and deck footprint, property lines, easements, the equipment pad, and the distance from the pool to each of them.
- **Engineered pool plans**, sealed by a Florida-licensed engineer, showing the shell, the steel, the depths, the plumbing layout and the equipment.
- **An electrical plan** for the equipment circuits, the lighting and the bonding grid.
- **Barrier compliance**, showing how the pool will meet the Residential Swimming Pool Safety Act before the final inspection.
- **Contractor license and insurance information** for the pool contractor and, where the jurisdiction asks for them, for the electrical and gas subcontractors.
- **Other sign-offs the lot triggers**: HOA architectural approval where the community requires it, a zoning or setback check, a septic-system review, or a flood-zone elevation review.

Our process front-loads this package. We survey the lot, model the pool against the setbacks and the code in 3D, and get the rendering approved by you before we submit. A clean, complete submission is the single biggest thing a contractor can do to keep the review moving.

## Electrical: bonding, GFCI protection and a separate inspection

Pool electrical work is governed by the National Electrical Code's swimming-pool article, which Florida adopts through the Building Code. Two things it requires show up on every residential pool inspection:

- **Equipotential bonding.** The steel in the shell, the metal in the deck, the pump, the heater and the light niches are all tied into one bonded grid so that nothing around the water can be at a different electrical potential. The inspector wants to see this grid before the deck is poured, because afterwards it is invisible.
- **Ground-fault protection** on the pool circuits, and defined clearances between the water and outlets, lights and overhead lines.

The electrical scope is permitted under the pool permit or, in some jurisdictions, as its own sub-permit. Either way it has its own rough-in inspection and its own final, by a licensed electrical contractor.

## Plumbing and gas

Pool plumbing is inspected under pressure before it is buried. The inspector checks the suction and return runs, the suction outlet (main drain) covers, which must meet the anti-entrapment standard the code references, and any connection to the home's water supply, which needs backflow protection. A gas heater adds a gas line, and the gas work is permitted and inspected as gas work, by a contractor licensed for it.

## Zoning, setbacks, easements and the HOA

The building department checks the pool against the Building Code. **Zoning** checks it against your property's rules: how far the pool and the equipment must sit from property lines, whether it can sit in a utility or drainage easement (usually not), and what the lot coverage limits allow. These distances are set by each city or county and vary by zoning district, so there is no statewide setback figure that is worth quoting. Your site plan either passes or needs a variance, and a variance is a separate, slower process.

If you live in a deed-restricted community, the **HOA** architectural review is a separate approval. The building department does not enforce HOA rules, and the HOA does not issue permits, but a project that skips the HOA step can be stopped by the HOA after the permit is issued. We ask for the HOA packet at the first meeting.

## Septic systems and wells

This is the item that catches North Florida homeowners most often. Many lots in Alachua, Marion and the surrounding counties are on a septic system rather than sewer. The drainfield and the tank have required separation distances from a pool, and the onsite sewage program (the state agency that regulates septic systems) reviews whether the pool location is acceptable or the system has to be modified. A private well adds its own separation rules. Both are settled before the building permit, not during construction, which is why we locate the tank and drainfield on the first site visit. ## Flood zones and coastal properties

If any part of the lot is in a FEMA special flood hazard area, the jurisdiction's floodplain rules apply to the pool and, more often, to the equipment pad, which may have to be elevated. Coastal lots can also fall under the state's coastal construction control line, which adds a separate Department of Environmental Protection review. Both are visible on public maps before you design anything, and we check them during the site evaluation.

## Safety barriers and the final inspection

Florida's Residential Swimming Pool Safety Act (Chapter 515, Florida Statutes) applies to every new residential pool. Before the pool can pass its final inspection, it must have at least one of the safety features the statute lists. The options include a barrier that meets the Building Code's residential pool barrier requirements, an approved safety pool cover, exit alarms on every door and window with direct access to the pool, and self-closing, self-latching devices on those doors. Most of our clients meet it with a barrier, a screen enclosure, or both.

What the code asks of a barrier, in plain terms:

- It must be at least 48 inches high, measured on the outside, with no gaps or footholds that would let a small child climb or pass through.
- Gates must open outward, away from the pool, and be self-closing and self-latching, with the latch positioned so a small child cannot reach it.
- Where the house wall forms part of the barrier, the doors and windows in that wall have to be protected with alarms or self-closing, self-latching hardware.
- A screen enclosure can serve as the barrier when its doors and screens meet the same requirements.

The barrier is checked at the final inspection, which is why a pool that is otherwise finished can sit unused for weeks while a gate latch or a door alarm is corrected. We plan the barrier with the pool, not after it.

## The inspection sequence

Inspections are called in by the contractor as each stage is ready, and the next stage cannot be covered until the previous one passes. The order is set by what has to be visible:

1. **Layout and excavation** on some jurisdictions, confirming the pool is where the site plan says.
2. **Steel and plumbing** before the shell is shot: the rebar grid, the bonding, and the plumbing under pressure.
3. **Shell** after the gunite or shotcrete, where required.
4. **Electrical rough-in** before the deck covers the conduit and the bonding connections.
5. **Deck and equipment** as those are completed.
6. **Barrier and final**, including the electrical final, after which the permit is closed.

Each jurisdiction's checklist differs slightly, and some combine stages. The exact list for your address is printed on the permit when it is issued. ## The Notice of Commencement and what you sign

For construction above the value threshold set in Florida's construction lien law, a Notice of Commencement is recorded in the county records before the first inspection and a certified copy is posted at the site. It is the document that makes the lien law work for the owner and the people who supply the project, so do not treat it as paperwork. You also sign the permit application as the owner. Read both; we walk you through them.

## What slows a pool permit down

In our experience, delays come from a short list and almost never from the review itself:

- An incomplete package, usually a missing HOA approval, a site plan without easements, or unsigned engineering.
- A septic drainfield or well separation that was discovered after the design was done.
- A setback or easement conflict that needs a redesign or a variance.
- A barrier plan that was left to the end.

Review times vary by office and by season. We do not publish a number because the honest one changes month to month; ask us what the office covering your address is running at the moment, and we will tell you.
