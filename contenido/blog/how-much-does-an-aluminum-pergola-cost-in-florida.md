Our estimator prices an aluminum pergola at **$8,500**. That number comes from the same approved price table the [pool cost estimator](/pool-cost-estimator) runs on, and it is a real figure for an ordinary structure: one pergola, ordinary post spacing, landing on ground that is ready to take it.

What it is not is a price per pergola. Six decisions move that line, and only one of them is size. The rest are structural, they are all made before anything is ordered, and they are the reason two pergola quotes can carry the same total and describe different buildings.

## What the $8,500 line actually is

It is a single line item, not a rate. The estimator does not ask how big the pergola is, the way it asks for pool and deck square footage — it adds one figure when you tick the box. That is a deliberate simplification and it holds for the ordinary case.

Tick it on the estimator's default configuration and the range moves from **$83,000 – $102,000** to **$91,000 – $111,000**. Which is the honest way to see a pergola in a backyard project: not as a separate purchase, but as roughly a tenth added to the yard.

Two things that line does **not** include:

- **The permit.** Our estimator calculates permits and engineering as 9 percent of the pool and deck. The pergola is not in that base.
- **Anything the pergola is meant to hold.** Fans, lights, screens, a heater, speakers. Those are separate, and what they cost later depends on what was run through the posts before they were closed.

## 1. Span, not square feet

This is the factor that moves the price most and the one homeowners almost never ask about.

People size a pergola by area. Aluminum is priced by what a beam has to carry across an unsupported distance. Take the same footprint and remove two posts to open one side, and you have not saved two footings — you have doubled the clear span, and the beam depth and the wall thickness of the extrusion both have to go up to carry it.

A structure with a post at every corner and one with a long open side can be identical on a floor plan and different on an order sheet. If you want an open side — because it faces the water, or because a post would land where the table goes — that belongs in the first design conversation, not in a change order after the members are ordered.

## 2. What it lands on

The pergola is only as cheap as the ground under it. In rough order, from least to most expensive:

1. **A pool project still under construction.** Footings go in before the deck is poured. This is the cheapest moment there will ever be, and it is covered in [the upgrades worth planning before construction starts](/blogs/pool-upgrades-worth-planning-before-construction).
2. **An existing concrete slab.** Workable, if it is thick enough and reinforced where the posts land. Sometimes it is, and sometimes the honest answer is a poured pad at each post.
3. **An existing paver deck.** Pavers come up, footings get dug and poured, pavers go back. The work is routine; the cost is the labor of doing a deck twice.
4. **Attached to the house.** Trades footings for a ledger connection, a flashing detail and a wall that can take the load. Whether attached or freestanding is the right layout is [a decision of its own](/blogs/attached-vs-freestanding-pergola); as a price factor, it moves money from the ground to the wall rather than removing it.

What the footing work involves in each case, and the point at which it stops being free, is the sequencing question in [the seven pergola decisions that close before construction](/blogs/pergola-design-decisions-before-construction). For reading a quote, only one thing has to be settled: which of the four rows above you are in. A contractor who has not asked is pricing a different one.

## 3. Wind load, which is set by the lot

Every permanent structure in Florida is engineered for a design wind speed, and that speed is set by where the lot sits — not by the state as a whole. A coastal South Florida site carries a stricter requirement than an inland North Florida one, and the practical effect is that the same drawing does not carry from one county to the next. Heavier members, more anchors, deeper footings.

For the quote, this has one clear consequence: **engineering is a real line, and sealed drawings are what the building department reviews.** A price with no engineering in it is a price for a structure nobody has checked. What the permit process itself looks like, county by county, is in [whether you need a permit for a pergola in Florida](/blogs/do-you-need-a-permit-for-a-pergola-in-florida).

## 4. Fixed slats or operable louvers

This is the largest single fork available, and the estimator prices both sides of it: **$8,500** for a pergola, **$18,000** for a motorized louvered roof. The gap is $9,500 — more than the pergola costs.

That money buys moving parts, a motor, controls and a rain path that closes, which changes the structure rather than just the top of it. It is a different build, not an upgrade tier, and [which one you should build](/blogs/pergola-vs-louvered-roof) is worth settling before you price either. The mistake to avoid is pricing a pergola, deciding later that you wanted the roof to close, and discovering that the posts and footings were sized for the lighter structure.

## 5. What runs through the posts

Aluminum posts are hollow, and the wire for lights, fans, retractable screens, a heater or speakers runs inside them and through the footings. Done while the frame is still open, it is a small line: conduit and an electrician's hours.

It is small only once. The same fixtures added afterwards mean surface conduit down a finished post, or a trench across a deck that is already laid — and that second bill lands against the deck, not against the pergola, which is why it never appears in the comparison people think they are making. You do not have to buy the fixtures up front. You do have to leave them somewhere to go.

Which provisions are worth leaving, and why it is settled at fabrication rather than at purchase, is [decision six of the seven that close before construction](/blogs/pergola-design-decisions-before-construction). Screens, lights and fans follow the same logic on the sides and overhead: [they are designed into the frame, not added to it](/blogs/designing-a-complete-louvered-roof-system).

## 6. Site conditions the estimator does price

Three of them, and all three are checkboxes rather than judgement calls. Two are percentage surcharges on the whole project, which means they move the pergola line even when the condition has nothing to do with the pergola:

- **Difficult access: 8 percent.** A gate too narrow, a slope, a septic field that cannot be crossed, a tree that stays. Our estimator applies it to the whole project, the pergola line included, so the same pergola that is $8,500 on an open lot is **$9,180** on that one.
- **Rock excavation: 12 percent.** The rock is under the pool, not under the pergola — but our estimator surcharges the project total the same way, so the pergola line reads **$9,520** with it ticked, and **$10,200** with both. Worth knowing before you read the pergola line on a pool quote as a pergola price.
- **HOA review: $1,500.** A flat line, and the fee is the easy part. The calendar cost is a board that meets when it meets, and the review normally has to clear before the permit application rather than alongside it.

## Which decisions are still open later

| Decision | Which way it moves the price | Can it change after the build? |
|---|---|---|
| Clear span between posts | Up, and steeply past the ordinary case | No — it sets the members |
| Attached or freestanding | Sideways: footings against a wall connection | No |
| Fixed slats or motorized louvers | $8,500 against $18,000 in our estimator | No — the footings differ |
| Conduit in posts and footings | A small line now, a large one later | Technically yes, cleanly no |
| Powder-coat color and hardware | Modest, and finish-dependent | No, without refinishing |
| Fans, lights, screens, heater | Separate purchases, any time | Yes, if the conduit is there |

## How to tell two pergola quotes apart

Two totals within a few hundred dollars of each other usually mean two different buildings. Ask both:

- **How many posts, and what is the longest clear span?** The answer explains most of the price difference on its own.
- **What is the wall thickness of the extrusion?** "Aluminum" is not a specification. Two structures of identical dimensions can be built from very different members.
- **Are engineering and sealed drawings included, or extra?**
- **Who pulls the permit and meets the inspector?** It should be the contractor, under their own license. A contractor asking you to pull an owner-builder permit is transferring the liability for the structure to you.
- **How does it anchor, and what happens to the existing deck?** Lifting and resetting pavers is normal work; it should also be a line rather than a surprise.
- **What is roughed in for power, and what is capped for later?**
- **What is the finish warranty, and on what exactly?** The structure, the powder coat and the hardware are usually three separate things. Why aluminum holds up the way it does in this climate — and what it holds up better than — is covered in [aluminum against wood over a Florida decade](/blogs/aluminum-vs-wood-pergolas-in-florida).

## When $8,500 is not the question you are asking

If the pergola is going beside a pool, over a kitchen, or in front of a screen room, it is not really a standalone purchase — it is one structure in a backyard that has to be laid out at once, because the footings, the conduit, the drainage and the deck all cross each other underground. [Planning a pergola beside a pool](/blogs/pergola-beside-a-pool-features-to-plan-together) and [the outdoor living design guide](/blogs/outdoor-living-design-guide-for-florida-homes) both start from the whole yard rather than from one structure, which is the order the ground actually requires.

## What to do with all of this

Run the [pool cost estimator](/pool-cost-estimator) twice — once with the pergola ticked, once without — and look at what it does to the total rather than at the line on its own. What comes out is a **plus or minus 10 percent** range built from the same approved price table as every figure above. It is meant to tell you which of your decisions is moving your number, not to quote your project.

Then, for a real figure, the span, the anchoring and the wind design all have to be looked at on site.
