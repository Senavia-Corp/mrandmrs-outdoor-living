The house usually decides this, not taste. Attach the pergola when the space you actually use touches the house **and** the wall behind it has something structural to bolt through. Build it freestanding when either of those is missing, or when the spot you really sit in is out in the yard. Everything else — material, roof type, finish — is a separate decision you make after this one, and material in particular is covered in [aluminum vs. wood over the long term](/blogs/aluminum-vs-wood-pergolas-in-florida).

The reason the structural question comes first is that on something this light, the load that governs is wind trying to lift it, not weight pressing down. An attached pergola sends that uplift into the house. A freestanding one sends it into its own footings. That single difference is what turns a layout preference into an engineering constraint.

| If this is true on your site | The layout usually picks itself |
|---|---|
| The wall has block, a tie beam or framing to bolt through, and the beam fits between the door head and the soffit | Attached |
| The eave is low, the wall is stucco over foam, or it is a gable end with nothing continuous at that height | Freestanding, or freestanding tucked under the eave |
| The place you actually sit is more than a few feet off the house | Freestanding |
| Pool plumbing, a drainfield or an easement sits where posts would land | Attached, or move the structure |

## What "attached" really means on site

A ledger, bolted through into structure: anchors into block or into the tie beam on a masonry wall, into studs or a band on a frame wall. Every one of those bolts is a hole through the weather barrier that has to be flashed and sealed so water does not end up inside the wall.

That detail is most of the job. An attached pergola is a waterproofing problem as much as a structural one, and it is where the shortcuts show up years later as a stain on the inside of the wall.

The failure we see in the field is simpler than that: a ledger lagged into fascia board, or into the foam trim under the stucco. Fascia carries a gutter. Foam trim carries nothing. Neither of them is a place to hang a structure that the wind is going to pull on.

## What "freestanding" really means on site

Its own footings, one under each post, sized and placed by whoever engineers the structure. The uplift goes to the ground through anchors set in that concrete. Nothing is bolted to the house, no one has to discuss what the tie-in does to a roof warranty, and the whole structure can go wherever the setbacks allow.

The price of that freedom is that you have to know what is underground. Posts landing on pool plumbing, an irrigation main, a septic drainfield or a utility easement either move the structure or stop it. That is a site walk with locates, not something you settle from a rendering.

## Five things on the house that decide it before you do

### The door head

The beam of an attached pergola has to clear the top of the slider or french door it passes over, and it has to tuck under the soffit. On a single-storey Florida house those two lines are often only a few inches apart. If the beam will not fit between them, attached is finished as an option — no design gets you around it.

Measure both heights before anyone draws anything. That gap is your entire attached option, expressed as a number.

### What is already on the wall

Walk it and write down what is there: windows, the condenser, the electrical panel, the gas meter, the hose bib, the dryer vent. Building over a condenser means roofing over equipment that needs clearance and service access.

Building over a window means the room behind it gets permanently darker. That is the consequence homeowners discover last and regret most, and it is the reason a fair number of attached pergolas should have been freestanding by six feet.

### Where the roof sheds water

An attached pergola sits directly under the edge where the roof dumps. In an afternoon storm that is a sheet of water landing on the rafters and then on the deck in one line, in the same place, every time.

It is solvable with a gutter and somewhere for the water to go. It is solvable cheaply at design time and expensively afterwards, once the deck is down and the grading is set.

### What is under the post locations

For a freestanding structure this is the whole feasibility study. Stake the post positions and have them located before the layout is final, not after the footings are dug.

### Setbacks, lot coverage and the HOA

Both layouts are structures, and both go through permitting and inspection; what varies is where each is allowed to sit. An attached pergola usually lands inside the footprint you are already permitted to build in. A freestanding one out in the yard can walk straight into a rear or side setback.

A structure standing alone in the middle of a yard also draws more architectural comment from an HOA than one that reads as part of the house. Our estimator carries an HOA submission as a $1,500 line, which is worth knowing before it arrives as a surprise. What the review itself involves is in [do you need a permit for a pergola in Florida](/blogs/do-you-need-a-permit-for-a-pergola-in-florida).

## The shade argument nobody makes: which way the rafters run

An open pergola only shades while the sun is high enough for the rafters to cast onto each other. In Florida that makes it a midday tool. By late afternoon the sun comes in almost sideways, under the roof entirely, and rafter direction stops mattering at all.

Attached fixes that geometry for you. The rafters span from the ledger out to the front beam, perpendicular to the wall, because that is the only way the structure works. Freestanding lets you turn the whole thing: orient the rafters against the worst sun, or set it far enough off the house that the house itself blocks the late light.

If the area you are covering faces west, understand before you commit that an open top will not solve four o'clock in either layout. That is a roof decision rather than a layout one, and it is the subject of [pergola vs. louvered roof](/blogs/pergola-vs-louvered-roof).

## Height is the thing attached gives up

An attached pergola's height is dictated to you by the soffit above and the door head below. A freestanding one lets you choose it, and that choice does more work than it looks like.

Higher feels airier, moves more air, and lets more low sun underneath. Lower shades better late in the day and traps more heat. In between sits the clearance a ceiling fan needs, which on a Florida patio is the single most effective comfort item you can hang — and which an attached pergola sometimes cannot fit under a low eave.

## What each one actually costs you

Our estimator prices a pergola at $8,500 as its base line, and what we show is that figure plus or minus 10%. It does not split by attachment, because the difference is site-specific:

- **Attached adds** the ledger, the flashing and sealing detail, sometimes blocking or an engineered connection into the wall, and a conversation with a roofer if the tie-in touches the roof.
- **Attached saves** two posts and two footings.
- **Freestanding adds** those two posts and footings — and the line nobody puts on a comparison sheet, the utility run.

That last one is the real divergence. Lights and fans against the house are a few feet from an existing circuit. Out in the middle of the yard they are a trench, conduit, and often a dedicated circuit. Our estimator prices LED fixtures at $450 each; that number does not change with distance. Getting power to them does.

So the honest summary is that the two layouts cost roughly the same in structure and separate in two items most quotes bury: wall detailing on one side, trenching on the other. ## The layout most Florida houses end up with

Self-supporting, tucked under the eave. Four posts on their own footings, the beam set a few inches clear of the fascia, with a trim closure if you want the line to read clean from the yard.

It looks attached and behaves freestanding: no penetrations through the wall, no ledger to engineer, no roof warranty to argue about. Roof water still has to be handled, but it lands where you planned for it instead of onto a structure bolted into the wall it runs off.

It costs two more posts and two more footings than a true attached pergola. On most projects that is the cheapest problem on the list.

It stops working when the eave is low enough that the beam cannot fit under it and still clear the door head. At that point you are choosing between a true attached structure at the only height that fits, or moving off the house entirely — which is exactly the decision this article started with.

## If screens might come later

An attached pergola gives you three sides to screen, because the house is the fourth. A freestanding one needs four, plus a header detail at the top of that fourth side.

Decide it now even if you build the screens later. The post spacing and beam depth a screen track needs are cheap to build in and expensive to retrofit. [When to add motorized screens to a pergola](/blogs/when-to-add-motorized-screens-to-a-pergola-or-kitchen) covers what to leave room for.

## Beside a pool, two constraints get added

Footings cannot land on pool plumbing or the equipment runs, which narrows where a freestanding structure can go on a yard that already has a pool in it. And if there is an existing screen enclosure, the pergola lives either inside it, which the enclosure height rarely allows, or outside it, which changes the layout.

Both are workable when they come up before the layout is drawn. [Eight features worth planning together](/blogs/pergola-beside-a-pool-features-to-plan-together) goes through the rest of the pool-adjacent list.

## How to settle it this weekend

1. Sit where you actually sit. Not where the pergola would look best from the kitchen window.
2. Measure from the patio to the top of the slider, and from the patio to the underside of the soffit. Write both down.
3. Stake the four post positions you would want and find out what is underneath them.
4. Watch the roof edge during the next afternoon storm and see where the water lands.
5. Then call about setbacks and, if you have one, the HOA.

A designer does all five on a site visit anyway. Doing them first changes what that visit is about: you arrive choosing between two layouts that both work, instead of being told which one does not.
