Building a pool in Florida is a design-build project with a fixed order: evaluate the lot, design and engineer the pool, permit it, build it in inspected stages, and start it up. The price is decided in the design, not in the construction, and the schedule is decided by the permit, the weather and the sequence of inspections, not by how hard the crew works. This guide walks the whole path so you know what is being decided at each step and what you can still change.

If you want the numbers first, read [how much a custom pool really costs in Florida](/blogs/how-much-does-a-custom-pool-cost-in-florida) and try the [pool cost estimator](/pool-cost-estimator). If you want the calendar, read the [construction timeline](/blogs/pool-construction-timeline-in-florida-what-to-expect-from-start-to-finish). This page is the map that connects them.

## What makes Florida different

Three things shape every pool project in this state and most of the decisions below come back to them.

- **Water and soil.** Much of Florida has a high water table, and North Central Florida adds limestone close to the surface on many lots. Both affect excavation, the engineering of the shell, and whether the pool needs a hydrostatic relief valve. A soil evaluation before the design is cheap insurance.
- **Weather.** A long summer rainy season and a hurricane season that runs through November. Rain stops excavation and concrete work; a storm can pause a project for a week. This is why the dry months are the busy months for pool builders.
- **Code and licensing.** The Florida Building Code, currently the 8th Edition (2023), and the Residential Swimming Pool Safety Act set what a pool and its barrier have to be. The Department of Business and Professional Regulation licenses the contractors who may build one. Both are enforced locally by the city or county building department.

## Step 1: site evaluation

Everything starts with the lot. Before a line is drawn we look at:

- where the pool can legally sit: property lines, setbacks, utility and drainage easements, and HOA rules;
- where water comes from and goes: grading, drainage, the water table, and on North Florida lots whether there is a septic drainfield or a well and where;
- what is under the surface: a probe or a geotechnical look for rock or unstable fill;
- how equipment and trucks get in: the access path decides whether a fence section comes down, a tree is protected or a smaller excavator is needed;
- where the equipment pad can go so that it is close to the pool, reachable for service and out of your sight line.

Our guide to [what to decide before you build a pool in Florida](/blogs/before-you-build-a-pool-in-florida) lists the twelve decisions that come out of this visit.

## Step 2: design, 3D modeling and engineering

The design turns the lot evaluation and your brief into a pool you can see. We model the pool in 3D against the actual lot so that setbacks, the deck, the spa, the equipment pad and the house all sit where they will really be, and you approve that rendering before anything is submitted. The design fixes:

- the shape, size and depth profile;
- the spa (attached, raised, or none), the sun shelf, the steps and benches;
- the interior finish, waterline tile and coping;
- the deck material and footprint;
- the equipment: pump, filter, heater, chlorination, automation, lighting;
- water features, and anything structural they need.

Once the design is approved, a Florida-licensed engineer seals the structural plans. That seal is what the building department reviews. Changing a feature after the seal means re-engineering, which is why we push the "what do you actually want" conversation to the front.

## Step 3: permitting

The licensed contractor applies for the building permit at your city or county, with the site plan, the engineered plans, the electrical plan and the barrier plan. Zoning, HOA, septic and flood-zone approvals are gathered alongside. We explain each piece in [which permits a Florida pool needs](/blogs/what-permits-are-required-for-pool-construction-in-florida). From your side, the permit stage is mostly waiting, and the length of the wait depends on the office, the season and how complete the package was.

## Step 4: construction, in order

A concrete pool is built in a sequence, and each stage is inspected before the next can cover it.

1. **Layout and excavation.** The shape is painted on the ground, and the hole is dug to the engineered profile. Rock or a high water table shows up here, which is why we checked for it in Step 1.
2. **Plumbing and steel.** The suction and return lines, the drains and the spa plumbing are run and pressure-tested; the rebar grid is tied to the engineered spacing. The bonding grid for the electrical system is tied in. Inspection.
3. **Shell.** Gunite or shotcrete is sprayed over the steel to form the structure, then cures. The pool now exists.
4. **Tile and coping.** The waterline tile and the coping go on the cured shell.
5. **Deck and electrical rough-in.** Conduit, lighting and the equipment circuits go in; the deck is formed and poured or the pavers are set. Inspections for electrical and deck.
6. **Equipment.** Pump, filter, heater, chlorinator, automation and lighting are installed and wired.
7. **Interior finish.** Plaster, quartz or pebble is applied, and the pool is filled immediately after.
8. **Startup, barrier and final.** Water chemistry is balanced, every system is run and tested, the barrier is in place, and the final inspections close the permit.

## Where the price is decided

A custom pool is priced from its own design. The levers, in rough order of weight:

| Decision | Why it moves the price |
|---|---|
| Size and shape | More square footage is more steel, concrete, finish and deck; complex shapes add forming and finish labor |
| Interior finish | Plaster, quartz or pebble differ in material cost and in how long they last |
| Spa | An integrated spa adds plumbing, heating capacity and its own structure |
| Deck | Concrete, pavers and travertine sit at different price points and sizes compound them |
| Equipment | Heater, salt chlorination, automation and lighting are each a line item |
| Site conditions | Rock excavation, difficult access, drainage work and septic modifications are the costs the lot adds |
| Permits | Set by the jurisdiction as a share of the project |
| Outdoor living | A pergola, a screen enclosure or an outdoor kitchen built with the pool |

Our estimator prices these from an approved table we use in real proposals, so the range it shows is the range we quote from. We do not publish generic "a pool in Florida costs X" figures because they are wrong for any specific lot. After design, you get a fixed project price in writing before you commit.

## Where the schedule is decided

The calendar is set by four things: the permit review, the weather, the inspection sequence, and the cure times of concrete and plaster that no crew can shorten. We lay out each phase, what gates it and what can run in parallel in the [pool construction timeline](/blogs/pool-construction-timeline-in-florida-what-to-expect-from-start-to-finish). Projects that start in the dry season tend to run more predictably.

## Choosing the builder

The contractor you hire decides more than any single feature. Verify the state license on the DBPR site, ask who the engineer is, ask to see the 3D design process, ask how change orders are priced, and ask for a fixed price in writing. Our list of [ten questions to ask before you sign](/blogs/how-to-choose-a-pool-builder-in-florida) covers the rest, including the ones that reveal how a builder handles the permit and the inspections.
