Most people choose a pool builder by collecting three quotes and picking the middle one. That compares prices, which is the one thing about a pool project that is easy to compare and the least predictive of how it goes.

These are the ten questions worth asking instead. None of them are gotchas — a good builder will have quick, specific answers, and the speed of the answer tells you nearly as much as its content.

## The three that matter most

If you only ask three:

1. **Is the license held by the company I am contracting with, and does it cover pool construction?**
2. **Who runs my job day to day, and how do I reach them?**
3. **How are changes priced and approved once work has started?**

Licensing tells you whether the work can be permitted and inspected. The second tells you what the next few months feel like. The third is where most disputes actually begin.

## 1. What license do you hold, and in whose name?

Pool construction in Florida is a licensed trade with its own classification. A general contractor's license is not the same thing.

Ask for the number, then check it yourself on the state's public lookup. Two things to confirm beyond "active": that the classification covers pool and spa work, and that the name on the license matches the entity on your contract. Work sold by one company and permitted under another's license is a real pattern, and it is the homeowner who is left holding it.

## 2. Who is actually on my job?

There is a difference between the person who sells the project and the person who runs it. Ask who that second person is, how many jobs they carry at once, and how you reach them.

A specific answer — a name, a phone number, a normal response window — is a good sign. "Someone will always get back to you" is not an answer.

## 3. Is this quote the same scope as the others?

Two pool quotes are almost never for the same thing. One includes the equipment pad relocation, another does not. One assumes standard access, another has walked the side gate.

Put them side by side and compare line by line, not total by total. Where one is silent, ask what happens if that item turns out to be needed. A builder who says "that would be a change order" is being straight with you; the number to be suspicious of is the one with no line items at all.

The decisions that move the price are covered in [what a custom pool really costs](/blogs/how-much-does-a-custom-pool-cost-in-florida) — worth reading before the comparison, so you know which lines to look for.

## 4. What is not included?

Ask directly. The honest list usually contains some of: utility relocation, unexpected rock, dewatering a high water table, HOA submissions, landscape restoration outside the work area, and the electrical service upgrade a heater sometimes needs.

None of those are red flags as exclusions. They are red flags as surprises.

## 5. How do change orders work?

The answer you want: in writing, priced, signed before the work happens.

Verbal changes that appear on the final invoice are the most common source of pool disputes, and the fix is entirely procedural. Agree at the outset that nothing changes without a signed order, and the rest of the project gets easier.

## 6. What does the payment schedule track?

Payments should follow completed stages, not the calendar. A schedule tied to inspected milestones means you are never far ahead of the work and the builder is never funding your job out of the next one.

Be careful with large deposits before anything is permitted, and with a schedule where the final payment is too small to matter — the last 5% is what gets the punch list finished.

## 7. Who pulls the permits?

The contractor should, under their own license. A builder who asks the homeowner to pull an owner-builder permit is transferring the liability for the work to you, and most people who agree to it do not realize that is what happened.

Permits also tell you about scope: pool, electrical, plumbing and any enclosure or barrier are separate reviews. [What permits are required for pool construction in Florida](/blogs/what-permits-are-required-for-pool-construction-in-florida) covers what a full set looks like.

## 8. What happens if an inspection fails?

Failed inspections happen. The useful question is what the process is when one does — who reschedules, how quickly, and whether the delay is absorbed or billed.

A builder who says inspections never fail is telling you either that they do not do many, or that they are managing your expectations rather than the project.

## 9. What is the warranty, and on what exactly?

A pool has several different warranties layered on top of each other: the shell, the interior finish, the equipment (manufacturer, not builder) and the labor. They have different lengths and different exclusions.

Ask for them separately and in writing. "Fully warrantied" as a single phrase covers nothing you can rely on later.

## 10. Can I talk to someone whose pool you finished two years ago?

Anyone can supply a reference from last month. The interesting call is with a customer far enough past completion to have had something go slightly wrong, because the answer you are listening for is how it was handled.

Ask that customer one question: when you called about something after the final payment, what happened?

## A note on comparing

It is tempting to treat this as a scorecard. It is more useful as a conversation. What you are really assessing is whether the answers are specific, whether they are consistent across the meeting, and whether the person answering seems to expect the question.

The builder who has thought about failed inspections, exclusions and change orders before you asked is the one who has run enough projects to have needed a process.
