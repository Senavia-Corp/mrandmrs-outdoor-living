The changes you actually see are the vertical ones: the waterline tile, the coping edge, and whatever stands up behind the water. The interior finish is usually the largest single line in a pool remodel and the one you notice least from the deck, because at standing height you are looking at reflected sky rather than at plaster.

That is not an argument for skipping the finish. It cannot be deferred once the pool is drained, and it sets the color of the water in every wide shot of the yard. It is an argument for sorting your list by what changes the view from the kitchen window and what only changes the drone photo. The ten below are sorted that way.

## First, how to read anyone's before-and-after pair

Most before-and-after sets are partly a remodel and partly a photography difference. Three patterns account for nearly all of it:

- **Different time of day.** The before is shot at midday with hard shadows and a flat sky. The after is shot at dusk with the lights on. That alone will carry a pair.
- **Different height and lens.** The before is a phone held at chest height, close in, so the pool looks stretched and cramped. The after is a drone, stepped back, where every deck looks generous.
- **Different condition.** The before has a dirty waterline, a limp cover and a hose across the deck. The after has a wet deck, clean water and furniture that was placed that morning.

None of that is dishonest, but none of it is the remodel either. Ask for one pair taken from the same spot, at the same height, at the same time of day. Whatever difference survives is what you are buying. Apply the same test to [our own project gallery](/gallery): the photos worth learning from are the ones shot from where somebody would actually be standing.

## The rule: rank by what stands up in your sightline

Standing on a deck, you see the water at a grazing angle, so most of what reaches you is reflection. Vertical surfaces reach you at full value: the tile band, the face of the coping, a raised wall, the screen frame, the house behind. Horizontal surfaces close to your feet — the deck — reach you at something in between, but they cover more area than anything else in the view.

So the honest ranking is not by cost. It is by how much of a change is standing up in front of you.

| Change | What you see standing on the deck | Where it lands in the budget |
|---|---|---|
| Waterline tile band | High. It frames the water at the line the eye tracks | Small |
| Coping edge and profile | High. It draws the whole outline of the pool | Small to medium |
| Deck surface and tone | High. Largest area in the view | Large. The material sets the rate |
| A raised wall or spillway | High. Often the only thing standing up | Medium to large |
| A raised spa | High. Elevation is the thing you see | Large |
| Tanning ledge or wider entry | Medium. Changes the shape of the water | Medium |
| Deck footprint and proportion | High. It decides whether there is room to sit | Large. The area sets the total |
| Interior finish | Low at eye level, high from above or at distance | Large |
| Lighting | Nothing by day, everything after dark | Small per fixture |
| What you take away or screen | High, and the cheapest item on the list | Smallest, unless it needs planting |

## The four you see first

### 1. The waterline tile band

The tile band is the single most reliable tell of a pool's age. Two things date it: the pattern, and the chalky calcium line sitting on top of it that no amount of brushing takes off.

It is also a small area. A band of tile is a narrow strip around a perimeter, which is why it delivers more visible change per dollar than anything else in a remodel. The decision worth making deliberately is height: a deeper band reads as a border and makes the pool look intentional, a thin one reads as trim. Whichever you choose, it is the element people photograph without meaning to, because it is exactly where the eye lands.

### 2. The coping

Coping is the edge you walk on and the line that draws the pool's shape. A worn bullnose concrete edge with chipped corners is what makes an otherwise sound pool look tired.

Two properties do the work, and only one of them usually gets discussed. The profile sets how sharp the outline reads. The overhang sets the shadow line under it, and that shadow is what separates the deck from the water in any photo taken in sunlight — a pool with a crisp line under the coping looks newer than the same pool without one. Neither the tile nor the finish compensates for an edge that is wrong.

### 3. The deck surface

The deck is the largest surface in almost every view of the pool, which makes it the biggest single lever on the after photo — and the biggest budget line that scales with area rather than with taste. Our estimator prices decking at $12 per square foot for concrete, $22 for pavers and $35 for travertine.

One note is worth more than the material choice: resist matching the house. A deck close in tone to the wall behind it gives the eye nothing to separate, and the result photographs as one flat field. Deliberate contrast is what gives a finished yard its depth — and unlike area, tone costs nothing to get right.

### 4. Whatever stands up behind the water

Most old pools have nothing vertical in the frame except the house and a fence. Adding one element that stands up — a rendered wall with scuppers, a raised planter, a fire feature, a pergola — changes the photo more than any surface swap, because it gives the eye something at full height to read.

It also adds movement and sound, which is the part that does not show up in any photograph and is the reason people keep sitting outside. The scope jump depends entirely on which one you pick: our estimator prices a pergola at $8,500, and a raised spa has its own line below. A rendered wall is priced by what it is built from and what it is founded on, which is why it belongs in the site conversation rather than in a table.

## The three that change the shape of what you are looking at

### 5. A raised spa instead of a flush one

A spa set flush with the deck disappears from every photo taken at standing height. Raised, it becomes the second thing you see after the water, and the spillway edge gives the pool a moving line. The difference in our estimator is $12,000 integrated against $18,000 with a spillway — the same appliance, a different object in the yard.

### 6. A tanning ledge or a wider entry

A ledge changes the outline of the water, which is the shape your eye actually reads. It also changes where people sit, which changes how the yard looks when it is in use rather than staged. This is structural work — the shell gets cut and formed — so it belongs in the scope from the start, not in a second visit.

### 7. How much deck there is, not what it is made of

This is the one item on the list that changes the after photo without anything in it looking different. A pool ringed by an even walkway reads as a corridor in every frame, because there is nowhere in the picture for a person to be. Widen one side enough for a table or a pair of loungers and the same water, the same coping and the same surface read as a room.

So walk the footprint with a tape before anyone quotes a surface: area drives the deck budget far more than material does, and it also decides whether the finished yard has anywhere to sit. Which side to widen is a sun-and-use question rather than a visual one, and it is worked through in [the decisions that make an old pool feel new](/blogs/pool-remodeling-decisions-that-transform-an-old-pool).

## The three nobody puts on the list

### 8. The interior finish

The finish is the largest visual change from above and from the far end of the yard, and the smallest one from three feet away. That is not a reason to spend less on it. It is the item that sets how many years you get before the pool comes offline again, and our estimator prices a pebble finish at 1.15 times plaster and a premium finish at 1.35 times.

It is a reason to stop treating the color sample as the centerpiece of the design meeting. Pick it for service life first. Whatever color you land on, the water will read as sky from where you stand and as the finish from the drone.

### 9. Light

A good part of what sells an after photo is shot after dark, and none of it is possible without fixtures. A pool light is not a lamp — it is a niche in the shell wall with conduit behind it — so the moment to add one is while the shell is open. Our estimator prices pool LEDs at $450 per fixture.

The visual logic is simple. One light centered in a pool produces a glowing rectangle. Several, plus anything lighting the vertical surfaces behind the water, produces a yard with depth. One rule for the night photo: the light should hit what stands up, not only the water.

### 10. What you take away

The cheapest change on this list is deleting things from the sightline. The equipment pad visible from the patio door. The white ladder and handrail on a pool that no longer needs them. The chain-link line into the neighbor's yard. The cleaner hose. The hose reel.

Screening the yard is where landscape spending earns its keep on the visual side: our estimator prices landscaping at $8,000, and a hedge that hides a pad and a fence line does more for the view than the same money spent on a surface upgrade. Much of what makes a finished backyard look finished is the absence of things, and that is the part no idea list covers because there is nothing to photograph.

## The order these have to happen in

Several of these lock each other: the tile band goes in while the shell is drained, and a light niche needs the shell wall open and the deck out for the conduit behind it.

That sequencing is a different axis from this one, and it is worked through in [what belongs in the scope while the pool is open](/blogs/what-to-upgrade-during-a-complete-pool-remodel). If your visual wish list has grown to a new spa, a new ledge, a full deck replacement and a raised wall, you are approaching new-build spending while keeping the one thing a remodel cannot change — the shape of the hole and where it sits in the yard. ## How to brief this so you get what you pictured

Ask for the design conversation in the order of this article rather than in the order of the quote:

1. **What stands up.** Wall, spa elevation, pergola, planting. Decide the vertical elements first, because everything else is built around them.
2. **The outline.** Coping profile, overhang, deck footprint and where the shape widens.
3. **The edges.** Tile band height and pattern, coping material.
4. **The fields.** Deck surface, interior finish.
5. **The dark.** Light placement, on the water and on what stands up.
6. **The subtraction.** Pad, rails, fence line, hose runs.

Then ask for one thing at the end: a photograph from the spot you stand in most often — usually the patio door — and a sketch or render from that same spot. Not the drone view. If the design does not work from the doorway, it does not matter how it looks from forty feet up, because that is not where you will ever be standing.
